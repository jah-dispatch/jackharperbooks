DATE: NOVEMBER 17, 2026 DISPATCH NO. 002 // CHARACTER INTERVIEWS

Interview with the men of Hemingway Falls

Step inside the minds of Brian Bellvue and Rick Dixon.

Q. What’s your honest first impression of the person on the other end of that rusted copper mailbox at Lake Hemingway?

Rick: That kid’s a pedigree pony, through and through. Everything’s been handed to him on a silver platter. I might have accumulated wealth, but at least I earned it from smart investing.

Brian: Rick’s kind of a cranky asshole, not gonna lie. But, I felt a sort of desperation from his first letter. I just won’t tell him that.

Q. Be honest: when you opened that mailbox and saw the reply, did you think you were losing your mind—or did part of you want it to be real?

Brian: Absolutely! And here I was evaluating a psych patient in the ER, all the while wondering if I was replying to somebody who wasn’t even there at the mailbox.

Rick: Given my mental health history, I thought it was total lunacy. But, I entertained the notion just purely because I had nothing else to lose.

Q. What are you running away from every time you get in the car and head toward Hemingway Falls?

Rick: Clyde Peters. That rusty son of a bitch is liable to turn anybody to running away from a chaotic journalism career.

Brian: Mmmmmm. 72 hour shifts at the hospital should say everything, right?

Q. If you had to survive twenty-four hours in the other guy’s job—overnight neuro residency vs. a fluorescent-lit newsroom under Clyde Peters—how fast would you get fired?

Brian: I’m honestly not sure. As much as I wanted to be a writer, that would have been done as me being my own boss. I kinda low-key hate authority figures.

Rick: Absolutely not! I can’t be trusted to keep plants alive, let alone human beings.

Brian Bellvue:

Q. Medicine or writing: if your dad and the family trust fund vanished tomorrow, which career are you actually choosing?

Writing, 100%. Saving lives is nice and everything, but it’s way too complex.

Q. You’ve had a rough week—from the Room 317 levodopa scare to dodging your mom in the ER hallway—so what’s the one thing keeping you tethered right now? (Besides Jake the cat, obviously.)

Wondering when Rick’s going to reply so I can feel a little bit less insane.

Q. Why write on your gran’s honeycomb stationery instead of just sending a text or journaling on your phone?

What’s texting again? This is 1999, after all. Rick’s the one with technological advancements.

Q. You just evaluated Elena in the ER psych bay and felt that strange click that something doesn’t fit the standard checkboxes—do you trust your medical training or your gut more right now?

I totally thought I was going berserk.

Rick Dixon:

Q. You call yourself the “King of the emotionally detached,” so why on earth did you pull out a steno-pad and write back to a stranger’s letter?

I felt I had nothing else to lose, and I was grasping at straws for my own emotional survival. At the core of it, I thought I was slowly losing myself in a long-time battle with Schizophrenia woes.

Q. Between the butter-knife-wielding raccoons, the Douglas-fir chicken sign, and that three-inch-beaked crow—what is your official review of Hemingway Falls hospitality?

That should sum it all up right there, yeah?

Q. Clyde Peters wants a safe, on-time human-interest piece, and you’re out in the woods chasing ghosts—what makes a story actually worth risking your job over?

Mmmmm. Clyde’s only interested in keeping a certain Colorado politician safe from election fraud allegations. So, there’s nothing else to discuss, frankly.

Q. If “An Unsolicited Reality Check” had to give one piece of advice to his younger self twenty years ago, what would he write?

Don’t rush into marriage and raising a daughter. Take care of yourself, because if you can’t do that, how can you expect to care for anyone else?

Q. What’s currently sitting in the passenger seat of your car?

Rick: The latest volume of TIME Magazine and a pack of Marlborough Reds.

Brian: A ham and cheese sandwich I should’ve thrown away last week!

Q. What’s your 2:00 AM stress snack or drink of choice?

Brian: Coca-Cola Classic and pretzels.

Rick: Probably a bottle of finely aged scotch! Well in all reality, probably a pint of Ben & Jerry’s Chocolate Chip Cookie Dough ice cream.

Q. Describe the town of Hemingway Falls in exactly three words.

Rick: Superior to Seattle.

Brian: Quaint, Charming, Friendly.

 

Excerpts from the men of Hemingway Falls

Rick:

There’s really nothing like a busy lunch hour in a steakhouse. Seated in a tall, dark leather booth at El Gaucho, my nose tickles with a scintillating satisfaction as I take in the aroma of slaughtered cattle. This is the prime spot for an average Wednesday, having just narrowly escaped another team huddle at The Seattle Examiner before the newsroom dispersed for lunch. But I’m not lucky enough to have dodged a lengthy email from him, summarizing said meeting with a concise threat about abstaining from touching local stories out of Western Colorado under the guise of avoiding partisan distractions.

So he’s taking the old Roy Cohn play, here. I see where this is headed and history’s bound to repeat itself. Again.

I smirk into my iced-cola, taking a large swig of sugar and carbonation. It teases my tastebuds, raring to consume a sixteen ounce ribeye straight off the bone. Nothing surprises me anymore. At least, well, I know how this story goes. It’s a slimy underbelly of the beast to be embedded in the media circuit with corruption and power dynamics when the editor-in-chief is none other than the cousin of a certain Mesa County Clerk and Recorder with hair dressed like a crusty Q-tip.

A dry scowl escapes my lips once I swallow the sip of Pepsi, heralding the server’s arrival with an air of anticipation on my visage. Finally, the moment I’ve been waiting for all morning. Death. The visceral satisfaction ignites from within as I thank the waiter for doing his job.

Finally. Come to papa, you poor, unfortunate son of a bitch.

The meat is so tender, seared only slightly to the point that fresh crimson jus pools around the slab. Second chance to the slaughter, courtesy of my jowls. With no time to spare, I don’t bother with the trivialities of using silverware. God gave us two hands and ten fingers, and I’m using my ferocious grip to summon all I have in me in order to rip the iron-rich meat straight off the bone.

Did I mention it’s busy here today? People can be seen scattered around every which way at tables and booths alike. Corporate juggernauts conducting client meetings with a side of sacrificial lamb, a slender man in a black suit with three women dressed like expensive hookers, and somebody’s always invariably kvetching about the weather.

Of course it’s raining, dipshit. This is Seattle for Christ’s sake.

After my bloody, tasteful meal, I step back out to the curb where I’ve parked my maroon Range Rover. It takes no effort sidestepping the heavy patter of liquid tears from the heavens as I slip inside behind the steering wheel. Next stop, Lake Hemingway.

On my drive out of the city, I’m nearly bumper-to-bumper with other vehicles lost in the slosh. It’s so grey and dreary out today that my mind finds itself lost amidst a sea of red and amber lights. And people are always in such a hurry.

 

Brian:

Mistakes happen every day. We forget to set our alarms for six in the morning. We drop our favorite mug full of hot coffee. And most of all, we’re doomed to repeat these errors because we’re simply human. And the worst of all these, when mistakes haunt us at work, when the critical moments bubble up beyond recognition, we often find ourselves asking what in the hell our efforts were truly for. Which brings me to my current quandary, standing here inside the doctors lounge at University of Seattle Medical Center with a styrofoam cup in my grasp and mailbox correspondence haranguing me about a critical error I made in room 317 last week—miscalculating an adult dose of levodopa for an eighty-six-year old male with Parkinson’s. Then again, it’s the least of these things that make us realize, we’re all bound to make the same mistakes over—and over.

It’s five of ten at night, and the sound of a roaring vending machine compressor takes hostage of my thoughts further as I recount the most harrowing display I’ve ever caused in the history of my residency thus far. The board is on my tail about such an error, as if I failed basic algebra in high school. But, you know what? Shit happens and there’s not much we can do about spilled milk once it’s soaked the linoleum and started to cause preemptive peeling at the corners it was laid. All I can do now is take a seat on a dismal leather sofa to wallow into this cup of stale coffee with the hopes I catch just an ounce of sleep while the third hospital floor is—well—we don’t ever dare say the ‘q word’ around here.

But oh how time doth flits and shits, my counterpart, Annie forgot the cardinal rule of overnight healthcare.

“How’s it going, Brian?” She asks, offering a look of pity. “What’s going on in that brain of yours on a quiet night like this?”

The fact she doesn’t realize she’s just jinxed our entire shift is baffling to me.

“Well, now you’ve gone and cursed us poor schlums stuck here in underpaid purgatory until seven tomorrow morning, haven’t ya?”

I don’t answer her question. Dare I never admit I’m fearful of losing my spot in the residency program over an error that, by all accounts, would never have happened if I wasn’t expected to pull seventy-two hour shifts on clustered hours of rest here and there, or expected to churn out mathematical formulas on a moment’s notice with no regard to the comfort and confines of our precious cranial system that’s just overtaxed.

At precisely this exact moment, my suspicion overturns itself at the sound of a page being called over the PA, “code blue—room 310. Code blue—room 310.”

Fuck. Just when I’d started to get a taste for coffee made in the prior decade.
